*Reclaiming Africa’s Spiritual Identity: Rebuilding the Institutions History Destroyed*

_The Spirit Champions Book Reading Club explores how the destruction of African spiritual institutions disrupted indigenous knowledge and why restoring them matters today._

*By Ayabonga Mayisela*

What happens to a people when the institutions that preserve their knowledge, culture and identity are destroyed? This question was at the centre of a book review session hosted by the Spirit Champions Book Reading Club, where members discussed Chapters 4 and 6 of *Restoring Africa’s Spiritual Identity* by African Hidden Voices (AHV).

The five reviews explored the historical disruption of African Indigenous Spirituality (AIS), the displacement of traditional spiritual authorities and the importance of rebuilding institutions that preserve African knowledge.

A recurring argument throughout the session was that restoring African spirituality requires more than individual belief. It requires institutions capable of teaching, protecting and passing indigenous knowledge to future generations.

• When knowledge was destroyed

In his review, *Where Knowledge Was Destroyed, Institutions Fell*, Mlandeli Nkululeko highlights the relationship between knowledge and the institutions responsible for preserving it.

Mlandeli writes: *”An institution is where a people's knowledge is gathered, guarded and passed on.”*

The statement captures the central argument that African spiritual institutions served purposes beyond worship. They were also centres of learning, community development and cultural preservation.

The review argues that colonialism and foreign religious influences contributed to the destruction or transformation of these institutions, disrupting the systems through which African communities transmitted knowledge.

Similarly, Ayabonga Mayisela, in *Institutions Outlive Their Founders*, writes: *”An institution without its guides is an empty building, and guides without an institution are scattered voices.”*

Mayisela emphasises that restoring physical spaces alone is insufficient. Institutions also need knowledgeable people, organised structures and systems that allow indigenous knowledge to survive beyond individual generations.

• Remembering Africa’s spiritual heroes

Khayisile Siziba’s review, *Who Were Our Heroes?*, examines the relationship between African spiritual authorities, traditional leadership and cultural memory.

The review argues that the displacement of indigenous spiritual guides weakened the connections between communities, their ancestors and their traditional systems of leadership.

Siziba writes: *”To remember our heroes is to restore that partnership, and that is the work this book calls us to.”*

This perspective challenges readers to reconsider African history beyond political struggles alone, recognising the role spiritual institutions and traditional authorities played in shaping communities.

The discussion also raises questions about how African spiritual practices have been represented and whether their historical significance receives sufficient recognition today.

• Restoration through spiritual centres

In *Restoration through Our Spiritual Centres*, Khanya Koyana identifies the disconnection of Africans from their ancestors, the destruction of sacred spaces and the persecution of spiritual guides as significant aspects of the historical disruption of AIS.

Koyana writes: *”Africa entered a ‘Dark Age’ when foreign religious systems systematically suppressed AIS.”*

The review presents spiritual centres as one way of addressing this historical disruption. Such centres could provide spaces for research, teaching and spiritual development while preserving the knowledge associated with sacred places and traditional practices.

However, rebuilding these institutions would require more than physical structures. It would also require people with relevant knowledge, clear organisational systems and a commitment to passing that knowledge on responsibly.

• Spirituality, education and the media

Nobukhosi Hlekiso’s review, *A System That Fits Every Space*, extends the discussion beyond traditional spiritual centres to consider the place of African spirituality in contemporary society.

Hlekiso writes: *”Spirituality, in this view, is not opposed to knowledge. It is where knowledge begins.”*

The review challenges the separation of spirituality from education, science and media, drawing attention to the historical role of African mystery schools as centres of learning and spiritual development.

• Beyond remembrance

Together, the five reviews present restoration as a collective responsibility that extends beyond remembering the past. They call attention to the need for institutions that can preserve indigenous knowledge, strengthen cultural identity and make that knowledge accessible to future generations.

The discussion also raises important questions: How can indigenous knowledge be preserved without separating it from the communities that hold it? How can educational institutions recognise African knowledge systems? And what role can young professionals play in ensuring that these traditions remain relevant?

Ultimately, the book review session challenges readers to consider not only what was lost, but also what must be rebuilt.

Restoring Africa’s spiritual identity, as the reviewers suggest, requires sustainable institutions, committed knowledge holders and spaces where African traditions can be studied, understood and passed on.

*About the book:* *Restoring Africa’s Spiritual Identity* by African Hidden Voices (AHV).

*This article is based on five reviews presented at the Spirit Champions Book Reading Club session on Chapters 4 and 6.*
