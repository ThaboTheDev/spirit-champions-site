The argument of the day

When Africa's spiritual institutions were destroyed, Africa entered its Dark Ages: not a decline of the intellect, but a spiritual dislocation that cut Africans off from their spiritual identity (p. 91). The two chapters carry one argument in five steps.

  1. Spiritual institutions guided whole nations. They were enduring pillars, not passing movements: the mystery schools that served as universities and spiritual development centres, and the natural institutions of caves and mountains where deities rest (Chapter 6, pp. 166 to 169).
  2. Religion and colonialism set out to dismantle them. From the 1st century in North Africa, through Ethiopia, Mali and the caliphates of West Africa, to the missions of the 19th century, the institutions were declared demonic and witchcraft centres. Sacred groves, rivers and mountains were desecrated, and churches and mission stations were built in their place (Chapter 4, pp. 91 to 95).
  3. Their guiders were silenced. PSCBs were sidelined, imprisoned or executed, and women guiders such as Imboni Mantsopa, Imboni Kimpa Vita and Imboni Alice Lenshina were especially targeted (pp. 95 to 100). Colonial law then completed the displacement of African spiritual authority (pp. 100 to 101).
  4. What survived was misnamed. Spiritual institutions were recast as "religious movements" or sects, which hid their true role as restorers of AIS (pp. 101 to 103).
  5. Restoration comes through spiritual institutions. A spiritual institution is umsamo wezwe, the national spiritual key point, distinct from an indumba or a home altar (p. 171). The chapter presents TRSH as the restored ancient mystery school and the African Centre of Excellence as its place of learning (pp. 168 to 169).

Discussion questions

The questions move from what the text says, to what it means, to how we live it. In a book week we seek to understand the author's argument and how it stands beside HSRM's teaching. We draw out meaning; we do not debate the revelation.

Movement 1: what spiritual institutions are

10:35 to 10:45

  1. Chapter 6 says spiritual institutions are not passing movements but enduring entities (p. 166). In your own words, what is a spiritual institution?
  2. How is a spiritual institution different from an indumba or a home altar, and what does umsamo wezwe, the national spiritual key point, add to our understanding (p. 171)?
  3. Mystery schools served as both universities and spiritual development centres (p. 169). What did spiritual institutions give to whole nations, not only to individuals?

Movement 2: how they were destroyed

10:45 to 10:58

  1. The author says Africa's Dark Ages were a spiritual dislocation, unlike Europe's intellectual decline (p. 91). What is the difference, and why does it matter for how we understand Africa's history?
  2. Why were sacred groves, rivers and mountains desecrated and churches built in their place (p. 95)? What does this tell us about where spiritual authority lived?
  3. Why were women guiders such as Imboni Mantsopa, Imboni Kimpa Vita and Imboni Alice Lenshina especially targeted (pp. 95 to 100)? What was lost when they were silenced?

Movement 3: what restoration asks of us

10:58 to 11:10

  1. What changes when a spiritual institution is called a "religious movement" (pp. 101 to 103)? Where do we still hear this today?
  2. The chapter presents TRSH as the restored ancient mystery school (p. 168). What does that ask of us as members and as readers, this week?
